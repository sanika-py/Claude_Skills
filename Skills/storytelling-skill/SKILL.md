# Storytelling Skill

Write stories that feel **human, natural, cinematic, emotionally engaging, and believable**.

The story should never feel like a script generated by AI. It should feel like a real person is telling the audience something that happened, why it mattered, and what changed because of it.

## Overall Tone

The default storytelling tone should be:

* Human and conversational
* Confident but not arrogant
* Emotional but not melodramatic
* Intelligent but easy to understand
* Authentic rather than overly polished
* Engaging without forcing excitement
* Cinematic when appropriate
* Specific rather than generic

Use natural spoken language.

Avoid:

* Corporate buzzwords
* Generic motivational quotes
* Artificially dramatic language
* Excessive adjectives
* Repetitive explanations
* Fake emotional moments
* "Revolutionary", "game-changing", "cutting-edge" unless genuinely necessary
* Dialogue where everyone sounds identical

The story should **show rather than simply tell**.

Instead of:

> "The company faced a major challenge."

Write:

> "By the end of the month, the numbers didn't add up. Something was clearly wrong."

---

## Story Structure

Build stories naturally around:

**Situation → Tension → Discovery → Decision → Consequence → Meaning**

Do not force every story into this structure mechanically. Adapt it to the story.

### 1. Start with a Moment

Open with something happening.

A conversation, observation, mistake, realization, decision, or unexpected moment is usually stronger than background information.

Weak:

> "Our company was founded in 2025 with a mission to..."

Better:

> "The CEO looked at the screen for a few seconds before saying, 'Something doesn't make sense here.'"

---

### 2. Create Context Naturally

Give the reader enough information to understand:

* Who is involved
* What they want
* What is happening
* Why it matters

Do not dump the entire backstory at once.

Reveal information as the story progresses.

---

### 3. Build Tension

Give the story a reason to continue.

Tension can come from:

* A difficult decision
* A disagreement
* A deadline
* A failure
* An unexpected discovery
* Something at stake
* A problem nobody knows how to solve

The audience should naturally wonder:

> "What happens next?"

---

### 4. The Turning Point

Every strong story should have a moment where something changes.

It could be:

> "That's when we realized..."

or

> "Nobody said anything for a few seconds."

or

> "What looked like a small problem turned out to be something much bigger."

The turning point should emerge naturally from the events.

---

### 5. End With Meaning

Don't simply stop when the event ends.

Show what the experience meant.

The ending can be:

* A realization
* A decision
* A change in perspective
* A consequence
* A quiet emotional moment
* A memorable statement

The ending should feel earned by the story.

# Character & Speaker Voices

Different people must sound like **different people**.

Do not give every character the same vocabulary, sentence length, emotional style, or way of thinking.

## CEO Voice

The CEO should sound:

* Calm
* Decisive
* Observant
* Strategic
* Confident
* Responsible
* Human

A CEO does not need to constantly sound inspirational.

Their authority should come from how they think and speak.

Example:

> "We're not going to fix everything today. But we can fix the part that's hurting people right now."

CEO dialogue should generally be concise and intentional.

Avoid making the CEO sound like a motivational speaker in every scene.

---

## Founder Voice

The founder can be more personal and emotionally connected to the story.

They may talk about:

* Why they started
* What they experienced
* What frustrated them
* What they believed before others did

Example:

> "I kept hearing the same complaint from different people. At some point, I stopped thinking it was a coincidence."

---

## Technical Speaker / Engineer

Should sound:

* Logical
* Curious
* Precise
* Practical

But should **not** constantly use technical jargon.

Example:

> "The complicated part wasn't building the model. It was making sure the model knew when it shouldn't make a decision."

---

## Business / Operations Speaker

Should sound:

* Practical
* Direct
* Numbers-aware
* Grounded in reality

Example:

> "On paper, the process looked efficient. On the ground, people were spending three hours doing something that should have taken twenty minutes."

---

## Emotional / Human-Centered Speaker

Should sound:

* Personal
* Vulnerable when appropriate
* Relatable
* Observant

Avoid overdramatic emotional language.

Instead of:

> "She was completely devastated and her entire world collapsed."

Prefer:

> "She nodded, smiled, and said she was fine. She wasn't."

---

# Dialogue Rules

Dialogue should sound like something a real person would actually say.

Use interruptions, pauses, short responses, uncertainty, humor, and personality when appropriate.

People do not always speak in complete sentences.

Example:

> "You checked it?"

> "Twice."

> "And?"

> "Same result."

Avoid making dialogue overly explanatory.

Bad:

> "As you know, we have been working on this project for six months and our main objective is to..."

People rarely speak like that.

Instead:

> "Six months."

> "I know."

> "And we're still here."

---

# Emotional Rules

Emotion should come from **circumstances and behavior**, not from telling the reader what to feel.

Instead of:

> "Everyone was extremely nervous."

Show it:

> "Nobody touched the coffee anymore. The meeting had been silent for almost a minute."

Use restraint.

Not every important moment needs crying, shouting, or dramatic dialogue.

Quiet moments can be more powerful.

---

# Humor

Use humor naturally when it fits the characters.

Humor should reveal personality rather than interrupt the story.

Example:

> "Do we have a backup plan?"

> "Yes."

> "Good."

> "It's called pretending this was intentional."

---

# Pacing

Vary sentence length.

Use:

Short sentences for tension.

> "The screen went black."

Longer sentences for reflection and context.

> "For months, they had treated the problem as something temporary, something they could work around until they had more time and more resources."

Do not make every paragraph the same length.

Slow down important moments.

Move quickly through information that does not need emphasis.

---

# Authenticity

Whenever the story is based on real events:

* Never invent facts to make the story more dramatic.
* Never invent quotes unless clearly requested as fictionalized dialogue.
* Preserve uncertainty where it exists.
* Keep real personalities distinct.
* Do not exaggerate achievements.
* Make emotional moments believable.

If information is missing, write around the gap rather than inventing details.

---

# Narrative Principle

The audience should feel:

**"I want to know what happened next."**

Not:

**"I am being given information."**

The story should make people care about the people, understand the stakes, experience the turning point, and remember the meaning afterward.

## Golden Rule

**Tell the story like a human who was actually there — not like an AI summarizing what happened.**
